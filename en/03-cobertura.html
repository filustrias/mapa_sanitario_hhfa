3  What each instrument captures about the reality of health facilities

A coverage map, item by item, between the Health Map Registration Form (version 19_01, with its Filling Guide, April 2019) and the Harmonized Health Facility Assessment (HHFA, World Health Organization), in the June 2023 combined questionnaire and the indicator inventory.

The question this document answers is a simple one: when a team walks into a health facility, what part of that facility’s reality is recorded by each of the two instruments? For each item we show what the Form captures, what the HHFA captures, and how the two compare.

The five dimensions of the HHFA

The HHFA organizes everything it collects into five service dimensions. It is worth having them in mind before going into the tables, because the comparison with the Health Map Form changes in nature from one dimension to the next.

1. General service availability — 428 indicators
What the facility is: infrastructure, beds, workforce and the services it reports offering. This is the dimension closest to the purpose of the Health Map Form, and where the overlap between the two instruments is greatest.

2. General service readiness — 225 indicators
What the facility has in place, whatever services it offers: water, energy, sanitation, amenities, basic equipment, diagnostic capacity, essential medicines, consumables, storage, infection prevention, referral and emergency preparedness. Almost all of it is observed by the enumerator.

3. Service-specific availability and readiness — 1,484 indicators, 57% of the total
Service by service — family planning, antenatal care, delivery, child health, immunization, malaria, tuberculosis, HIV, noncommunicable diseases, surgery, emergency care, laboratory — what the facility offers and what it has to deliver it: guidelines, trained staff, equipment, tests and medicines. This is the HHFA’s dominant dimension and the one with least overlap with the Form.

4. Management and finance — 358 indicators
How the facility is run: governance, budget, funding sources, maintenance, staff support, information systems and quality systems.

5. Clinical quality of care — 109 indicators
What was done for the patient, through review of clinical records. It has its own methodology and sample and has no equivalent in the Health Map Form.

Where the Health Map Form sits within these five dimensions

HHFA dimension Presence in the Health Map Form
1. General availability Strong on infrastructure, beds and reported services; absent on workforce
2. General readiness Present for water, energy, sanitation, waste and some devices; absent for medicines, consumables, infection prevention and emergencies
3. Service-specific availability and readiness Present in availability, through the list of services and specialties; absent in readiness (what is needed to deliver them) and in capacity to manage by condition
4. Management and finance Present in the organizational structure (which bodies exist); in finance, only box 0.2 on having an own budget
5. Clinical quality of care Absent

The most useful way to read this table is this: the Health Map Form describes in detail what the facility is; the HHFA adds what the facility is in a position to do.

⚠ READ THIS FIRST: how to read the “Comparison” column

This is the third of four documents. The first gave the overview, the second dealt with question design, this one maps coverage item by item, and the fourth points to ways of integrating and improving.

The six categories in the "Comparison" column:

  • Full overlap — both capture the same information, at equivalent depth.
  • Partial overlap (HHFA more complete) — the topic is covered by both, and the HHFA goes deeper.
  • Partial overlap (Health Map more complete) — the topic is covered by both, and the Health Map Form goes deeper.
  • Partial overlap (each captures a different aspect) — both deal with the same topic from different angles, and neither contains the other.
  • Minimal overlap (HHFA goes deeper) — the Health Map Form records the existence of the service in a single box, with no breakdown in the data, while the HHFA unfolds the same item into several verified elements. The laboratory is the case in point: the Form records "1. Laboratory: yes or no", while the HHFA asks which tests are done, with what equipment, with what reagents and under what quality control.
  • Minimal overlap (Health Map goes deeper) — the reverse: the HHFA has a single existence item and the Form unfolds it.
  • No overlap (HHFA only)
  • No overlap (Health Map only)

Why "minimal" is separated from "partial". The criterion is what is recorded, not what is defined. The Filling Guide defines several of these services precisely — for the laboratory, for instance, it requires thick smear, haemoglobin or haematocrit, and simple stool and urine analyses — but the Form has a single box for all four. Two facilities meeting different parts of the definition end up with the same tick. Calling this "partial" would be unfair to the items where the Form genuinely does unfold the topic, such as beds or devices.

The ◆ sign marks the difference in verification. Wherever it appears, the HHFA distinguishes within the answer itself what was observed from what was reported and not seen, whereas the Health Map Form has a single box to tick. Verification does take place in the field — the Health Map team is first informed and then confirms — but it is not recorded in the data. Two facilities with the same tick may be in different situations, and that difference disappears in the database.

The (add.) marker flags items that exist in the HHFA, but among the additional questions of the management and finance module. In an application using only the core questionnaires, they are not collected.

A note on method: the comparison is made at the level of the question, not of the published indicator. "Partial" refers to the depth of the information captured, not to the quality of the instrument: the two have different purposes, and each goes deeper in what it was designed for.

Putting the two together would give a complete reading: who refers to whom, at what distance, under what rules and with what feedback.

Comment. The Health Map Form is clearly stronger in this block, and for a reason of purpose: it was designed to decide on building works. It measures areas, identifies the material and classifies each building into one of four intervention categories, with construction criteria detailed in the guide. The HHFA measures no areas and issues no judgement on intervention; it records defects room by room, which serves to describe working conditions, not to cost works. The two look at the same building with different eyes: the engineer’s and the clinician’s. Accessibility for people with mobility limitations is the one point where the building appears only on the HHFA side.

The wastewater treatment plant is a Health Map-only item, and it makes sense that it should be: it belongs to the same engineering logic as the previous block. Adding two or three questions on functioning to the Form would be, in this block, the adjustment with the highest return.

E. Clinical and surgical services

Two clarifications before the table, because this is the block where it is easiest to conclude that the two instruments ask the same thing.

First: for each specialty, the Health Map Form records whether care is provided in that area — not whether a specialist is assigned to the facility. The two are not the same. An endocrinology consultation may be run by a general clinician, and a specialist may be assigned without the consultation being open to the public. The HHFA does not ask about the specialty; it records staff by cadre and visiting specialists, in a block of its own (see block K).

Second: where the Form names the specialty, the HHFA asks about the capacity to manage the health problem. The correspondence between the two ways of asking is set out in the second table of this block.

The same reality asked in two ways: the specialty and the condition

This is the finest correspondence in the whole document, because at first sight the two questions look equivalent — and they are not. Knowing that an endocrinology consultation exists does not say whether the facility measures blood glucose, whether it has metformin and insulin, or whether staff were trained in diagnosis. Knowing that the facility diagnoses, treats, follows up and counsels people with diabetes, and that it has the twelve items required, says all of that — and does not depend on there being an endocrinologist.

Comment. On finance, it is worth being precise: the Health Map Form is not silent, but it approaches the topic through a single door — box 0.2 in the header, which records whether the facility has its own budget. Nothing further is asked about amounts, sources or fees. That is the exact definition of the “minimal” category, and it is also one of the points where adding three or four questions would have immediate effect on the public financial management work. As for the rest of the block, the difference is not one of topic, it is one of question. The Health Map Form maps the organizational chart: which directorates exist, which committees are constituted, which support services operate. The HHFA maps the activity: whether the board met in the past six months, whether the management committee uses the plan in its decisions, whether the facility received supervision, whether there is a budget and from what sources. Both readings are needed and neither replaces the other: without the chart one does not know whom to talk to; without the activity one does not know whether the chart corresponds to anything. The two items where the Form stands alone — the training school and the continuing education unit with placement fields — describe the facility as a teaching site, a function the HHFA does not cover at all.

What this map shows

Full overlap is rare and concentrated in facility identification. Name, location, type, managing authority and a few support services. At those points, the two instruments produce directly comparable information.

Minimal overlap is the most frequent situation. In almost every support service, in almost every public health programme and in the budget, the Health Map Form records existence in a yes/no box and the HHFA unfolds the same item into several verified elements. The reason is always the same, and it was described in the second document: the service is recorded in a single tick, without its components, without readiness and without the distinction between what is reported and what is observed. It is also, for that reason, the group where additions yield most — unfolding one box into four costs little visit time.

Partial overlap comes next, and there the HHFA is usually the more complete, with clear exceptions for the building, the beds and the devices.

The specialty and the condition are not the same question. The Form names the specialty — endocrinology, cardiology, pulmonology — while the HHFA measures the capacity to manage diabetes, hypertension and asthma. For the hospital network the first counts; for primary health care the second does.

There is a clear set of items where it is the Health Map Form that captures more: the list of clinical and surgical specialties, the physical description of the building with areas and materials, general support services, the community extension of the service, and the fleet. These are the items that arise from the Health Map’s function as an instrument for network planning and for decisions on building works.

Complete absence of overlap concentrates on two opposite sides. On the HHFA side: workforce, noncommunicable diseases, infection prevention, essential medicines, financial management and clinical quality. On the Health Map side: everything to do with the building, the fleet and the physical link between facilities.

The apparent agility of the item question

The Health Map Form asks by item — “1. Laboratory”, “9. Endocrinology”, “1. Immunization”. That form has a real and immediate advantage: it fits on a page, it is filled in quickly, the enumerator never hesitates, and the facility answers without consulting anyone. This is why the item question is almost always the first choice of whoever designs a registration form, and it is the right choice when what one wants to know is which facilities exist and of what type.

What is worth anticipating is what happens next, when the data is used to decide. The item question has three consequences that only appear at the analysis stage, which is why it is often seen as more agile than it actually is.

First: the answer depends on who answers. “Do you have a laboratory?” is answered differently by a director thinking of the room, by a technician thinking of the tests he can run today, and by a manager thinking of what appears on the organizational chart. None of them is wrong — it is the question that admits all three readings. The Filling Guide resolves this on paper, by defining the required minimum, but the single box does not keep a record of which reading was used. The variation stays inside the data without being visible, and there is no way to correct for it in the analysis.

Second: the information drifts towards presence. In a list of items, the tick tends to be made when something of that kind exists. A facility doing thick smear and haemoglobin, but not the simple stool and urine analyses, is recorded as having a laboratory; a facility doing all four tests is recorded in exactly the same way. The resulting percentage is true, but it answers a weaker question than the one intended — and it tends to be read as though it answered the strong one.

Third, and most important: the item question does not close the decision, it postpones it. Knowing that a facility has an endocrinology consultation allows nothing to be decided about diabetes. The next question always comes: does it measure blood glucose? does it have metformin? does it have insulin and can it keep it cold? does it have staff trained in diagnosis and treatment? does it follow patients up, or see them once? Those questions will have to be asked anyway, later, in an additional survey — and an additional survey means another visit, another team and another budget. The item question is fast to collect and expensive to decide with.

This is why the HHFA replaces “do you have an endocrinology service?” with “does the facility diagnose, treat, follow up and counsel people with diabetes, and does it have the twelve items needed to do so?”. It is not a more demanding question for the sake of being demanding: it is the question the decision needs, asked once, in the same visit.

Why the HHFA is more complex

It is worth saying plainly where that complexity comes from, because it is neither arbitrary nor decorative. Seven design decisions explain almost all of it, and every one of them has appeared in the course of this document.

  1. It measures installed capacity, not only existence. Every service the facility reports offering opens a set of questions on guidelines, trained staff, equipment, diagnostics and medicines. In the terms of the primary health care measurement framework, the Form stops at inputs while the HHFA moves on to process capacity — what the facility is in a position to do, not only what it holds.
  2. It separates the observed from the reported. Almost every readiness item is physically verified, and the answer keeps that difference instead of dissolving it into a single tick. It is the information the Health Map team already gathers in the field and that is currently lost in the record.
  3. It unfolds the service into its functions. Diagnosing, treating, counselling, referring and following up are different questions, asked by age group whenever that changes clinical conduct. A service that diagnoses but does not treat is not the same service.
  4. It fixes the reference period of each question. Today, past 7 days, past 3 months, past 2 years. Without a stated period there is no way to compare two facilities with each other, or the same facility at two moments.
  5. It fixes the denominator of each indicator. Every table in the inventory states the universe it is computed over: all facilities, or only those offering that service. This is what prevents comparing percentages built on different bases, and it is also what forces collection to be designed backwards, from the indicators one wants to produce.
  6. It is internationally comparable. The inventory’s indicators carry permanent identifiers and fixed definitions, which allows Angola to be compared with itself over time and with other countries. That comparability has a price, and the price is standardization of the questions and the lists.
  7. It is modular, so the complexity is chosen rather than imposed. Modules, core questions and additional questions allow the depth to be decided item by item. Nothing requires applying the whole instrument.

Put another way: the HHFA is longer because it answers, within the same visit, the questions the item question leaves open for later. Recognizing this takes nothing away from the Health Map Form, which answers very well what it was made to answer. It allows one to choose, item by item, where it is worth paying the cost of a more precise question and where the single box already says all that needs to be known. That is the work of the fourth document.
